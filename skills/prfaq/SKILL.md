# PR/FAQ

A PR/FAQ is a press release for a product that does not exist yet, written from its launch date, followed by the questions a customer and a decision maker will ask. The press release fits on one page. If it cannot make the hero customer want the product, the product changes before the document does.

Sources for the structure: Ian McAllister's 2012 Quora answer on Amazon's internal press releases, summarized with his section list by [Mequoda](https://www.mequoda.com/articles/audience-development/an-inspirational-press-release-template-from-amazon), and AWS, [Product Management at Amazon](https://aws.amazon.com/executive-insights/content/product-management-at-amazon/), which says the press release "must fit on a single page" and splits the FAQ into customer questions first, then internal ones. Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, *Working Backwards* (St. Martin's Press, 2021), describe the full process. The checks below that go beyond those sources come from reviewing drafts in this house style.

Use the checklist two ways. Authors tick it while drafting. Reviewers report each unmet item as a finding in the `unified-review` format (severity groups, inline diffs). The `logic` skill still applies to the argument, and CLAUDE.md's writing rules still apply to every sentence.

## Before drafting

- [ ] **Hero customer.** One customer, in one vertical, named as a role ("the grower who owns the arm"), not a segment list. Everyone else is supply, channel, or partner.
- [ ] **The CFO sentence.** One sentence the hero would repeat to whoever approves the purchase. It names an outcome they pay for: money, time, idle capacity, risk.
- [ ] **The one differentiator.** The thing only this product does, stated in the customer's words ("switches tasks without forgetting old ones"), not the mechanism ("continual learning").
- [ ] **Launch scope.** What ships on the launch date, for which hardware, markets, or accounts. What does not ship is for the FAQ.
- [ ] **Evidence the hero exists.** A Customer Zero, pilot, or design partner whose numbers the release can cite. If the only evidence is in another vertical, that vertical is the hero.

## Press release, line by line

Write the elements in this order. Each item names what the element must do, then what to check.

### 1. Headline

- [ ] Names the company, the product, and the hero's benefit: "[Company] Launches [Product]: [what the hero now gets]."
- [ ] **Benefit, not mechanism.** Ask of each clause: does it say what the customer does or what the product is, or what the customer gets? "Unlock a marketplace," "download skills," and "AI-powered" are mechanism. "Switches tasks in minutes" is benefit.
- [ ] **Prerequisites.** If the benefit depends on buying or installing something first, the headline says so ("Add One Device, and..."), so a time claim like "in minutes" attaches to the repeated step, not the purchase.
- [ ] Uses the product's own terms. If the store is the Skill Store, the unit is a skill, not a "job" or a "cartridge."
- [ ] No absolutes the evidence cannot back ("never," "every," "any").
- [ ] No filler verbs: unlock, empower, revolutionize, transform.

### 2. Subhead

- [ ] One to three sentences. Says who it is for and the outcome, in the order the customer meets it: the one-time step (buy, install), then the repeated step (download, switch), then the payoff.
- [ ] Carries the CFO sentence if the headline does not.
- [ ] Every feature it names is stated as what the customer gets. "Runs on the device" is mechanism. Its benefit (works without a network, data stays on site, no cloud fees) is a separate claim, so pick the one you will stand behind or cut it.
- [ ] Does not repeat the headline in other words.
- [ ] Launch scope ("available first for...") only if the body does not already say it.

### 3. Summary paragraph

- [ ] Opens with city and the future launch date.
- [ ] States what launched, for whom, what it does for them, and where to get it. A reader who stops here knows the product.
- [ ] Introduces each product term once, defined by what it does ("a Knowledge Cartridge, a downloadable skill certified for a standard workcell").
- [ ] The hero is the actor. Name the role ("growers browse the store"), not a generic "operators" or "users."

### 4. Problem paragraph

- [ ] The hero's problem, in the hero's terms, with what it costs them.
- [ ] Names what they do today and why it fails. "Current solutions are inadequate" is not a problem statement.
- [ ] Comes before the solution and before safety or compliance detail. The reader learns why to care first.
- [ ] Scale numbers are about the hero's market, not an adjacent one. A global count that is mostly another industry misleads.
- [ ] Market facts presented as true today have a source in the FAQ. Invented launch metrics are fine, since the release is fiction about the future.

### 5. Solution paragraph(s)

- [ ] Says how the product removes the problem, in the order the customer experiences it.
- [ ] Mechanism appears only as far as the reader needs it to believe the benefit. Architecture belongs in the FAQ.
- [ ] Says nothing twice. Two paragraphs that each walk through "download, calibrate, run" merge into one.
- [ ] **Platform launches.** Other sides of the platform (creators, developers, integrators, data contributors) appear as the reason the hero gets more over time, in one short paragraph. Their economics go in the FAQ.
- [ ] **The platform contract.** If the platform works only under a standard (a published spec, an API, a certified configuration), name it once, plainly. It is what lets the sides work together, so it is not fine print.
- [ ] Safety, compliance, and certification claims say what the product does and what the customer or installer still owns. "Guarantees compliance" is a claim legal will strike if compliance belongs to the installed system.

### 6. Customer Zero or proof paragraph

- [ ] A named customer (placeholder if real names cannot circulate) with before and after numbers on the hero's outcome.
- [ ] Shows the differentiator in action: a sequence, not a single metric. Example: crop A, swap to crop B, back to A at prior accuracy.
- [ ] **Physical feasibility.** Every part the story needs exists in the product or is named as the customer's. If a fixed arm works "across greenhouses," something moved it.
- [ ] Metrics are defined well enough to check (picks per hour including or excluding travel).

### 7. Leader quote

- [ ] Says why the customer is better off, in plain words. Not a lecture on the technology.
- [ ] No false universals ("every animal brain learns without forgetting"). State the bounded claim.
- [ ] Consistent with the product as described. A quote that says "chip" when the body says "edge computer" is two different companies.

### 8. How to get started

- [ ] The steps, in order, with who does each and how long it takes.
- [ ] Prerequisites (hardware, accounts, installer) are explicit.
- [ ] Time claims match each other. "Minutes" for a skill and "days" for a cell build can both hold if the release says which is which.

### 9. Customer quote

- [ ] The hero's voice, describing the outcome they got, in words they would use.
- [ ] Numbers match the proof paragraph (38% per pound versus "nearly 40%" per crate needs one unit).
- [ ] Mentions only what the release already introduced. A third crop that appears only in the quote raises a question the release never answers.

### 10. Partner quote (platform launches)

- [ ] A creator, integrator, or channel partner whose quote ends on the hero's outcome, not on the partner's business model.
- [ ] The partner works in the hero's vertical.
- [ ] No puffery labels ("premier," "leading").

### 11. Price, availability, call to action

- [ ] Leads with what the hero pays, all in: hardware, subscriptions, install. Ideally tied to the savings in the proof paragraph.
- [ ] Platform revenue splits get one sentence at most. The full split goes in the FAQ.
- [ ] Prices are consistent with who sets them ("creators set prices" and "starts at $6,000" need a stated floor).
- [ ] Ends with where to get it.

## Whole release

- [ ] Fits on one page.
- [ ] One hero, one vertical. Other verticals get one clause or move to the FAQ.
- [ ] Each concept has one name, used everywhere (skill, cartridge, Knowledge Cartridge: pick one or define how they relate).
- [ ] No jargon the hero would not use: replace "catastrophic forgetting," "TCP," "pipeline," "ecosystem" with what they mean for the customer.
- [ ] Every number appears once or agrees everywhere it appears.
- [ ] No claim the company cannot defend in front of legal, safety, or a skeptical customer: absolutes, guarantees, "unbypassable."
- [ ] Real companies and named people in invented quotes are replaced with placeholders before the document leaves the team.
- [ ] Reads as written for the hero, not for investors. Business-model detail that only an investor needs moves to the FAQ.

## FAQ

The FAQ has no fixed length. Put the most important questions first, and leave out questions that do not change the decision.

### Customer FAQ (first)

- [ ] How much does it cost, all in, and what does it replace?
- [ ] What do I need before I start, and who installs it?
- [ ] What happens when it fails, and who supports me?
- [ ] Is it safe, and who is responsible for compliance in my facility?
- [ ] What happens to my data?
- [ ] What works with it at launch, and what does not?

### Internal FAQ (second)

- [ ] Market size, with each figure sourced and the arithmetic shown.
- [ ] Unit economics and what has to be true for them to hold.
- [ ] The hardest technical risk and the evidence it is solved.
- [ ] Why now, why us, why it does not already exist.
- [ ] Each side of a platform: why it shows up first, how the cold start ends.
- [ ] Who owns contributed data, who is paid for it, and how the people who generated it (crews, workers, users) are treated.
- [ ] Risks and what would make the team stop.
- [ ] Every answer answers the question asked, with numbers that match the press release.
