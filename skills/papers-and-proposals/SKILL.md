# Papers and proposals

Sources: Mensh and Kording, "Ten simple rules for structuring papers," *PLOS Computational Biology* 13(9): e1005619, 2017 ([doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619), CC BY 4.0), and the paper, grant, and proposal posts at [bitsandbrains.io](https://bitsandbrains.io/). Use the `documents` skill for equations, citations, and tables, and the `figures` skill for figures.

## The central contribution

- Focus the paper on one central contribution, the message a reader should recall a year later. The title states it, ideally as the one-sentence main result. Draft the title and the main figure first.
- Write for a reader who does not know the work and has to be convinced to care. Define terms, avoid unexplained abbreviations, and keep the number of open threads small.

## Context, content, conclusion

- Use context, content, conclusion at every scale. The paper: introduction, results, discussion. A paragraph: a topic sentence that sets context, then the new content, then a takeaway sentence. Context answers "why am I told this?" and the conclusion answers "so what?"
- Cover each subject in one place. Do not zig-zag back to it. Express parallel points in parallel sentence structure.

## Sections

- **Abstract.** A complete story: open broad, narrow to the specific gap, state what was done, give the key results, and close by answering the question, optionally with the broader significance. For a summary or abstract, the sequence is opportunity, challenge, gap, action, resolution. Spend two sentences on the main figure's result, one on each other figure, none on results without a figure.
- **Introduction.** Why the paper matters. Paragraphs narrow from the field's gap to the subfield's gap to the specific open question, then end with a short statement of how the paper fills it. Not a broad literature review.
- **Results.** A sequence of statements, each supported by a figure, that connect logically to the central contribution. Subsection headers and figure titles state conclusions. Each paragraph opens with the question it addresses and ends with its answer. A results paragraph for a display item gives the setting, the details, then the take-home sentence.
- **Discussion.** How the gap was filled, the limits of the interpretation and how future work could address them, and how the paper moves the field. Related work and extensions, not a summary of the results.
- Number every display item (figure, table, theorem) and reference it in the text.
- Credit the prior work the paper builds on. Its authors are likely readers and reviewers.

## Process

- Spend effort where readers are: the title, abstract, and figures reach the most readers, the methods the fewest.
- Outline first, one informal sentence per planned paragraph. Check that each paragraph advances the story before drafting prose.
- Treat writing as iterative. Be willing to discard and rewrite whole paragraphs. Test whether the outline can be told to a colleague in a few minutes, and use test readers and reviewers to find where the logic breaks.

## Grants and proposals

- Aim titles are action titles with a deliverable verb. Avoid "investigate" and "explore".
- One bold sentence per aim states its objective. Each aim gives expected outcomes, and pitfalls with alternatives.
- Distinguish the applicant's own work from others' at every citation.
- A one-page summary covers opportunity, significance, gap, challenge, action, and feasibility.

## Rebuttals

- Quote each reviewer comment, answer directly under it, and quote the revised manuscript text.
- Concede what is right. Ask the user before conceding a point you think is wrong.
